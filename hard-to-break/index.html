The story behind the song

It started with a fact. Concrete is crushed stone.

The thing holding up every building you've ever stood in is broken rock, bound and poured. Nothing standing anywhere wasn't broken first. That fact is the whole song. The veteran it came from never said a word about concrete.

What he said was this. You're going to break more than you succeed. A deployment teaches you lessons that break you, and when you get home you can accept being broken or you can build on it. His exact words were "you got to allow it." That's the part the song is built on. It isn't automatic and it isn't a slogan. It's a choice, and you make it more than once.

Ain't nothing standing anywherethat wasn't broken first.

He'll tell you he's hard to break because he's been broken so often. He'll also tell you that when he does break, it gets bad. Both are true, and both are in the song, which is why the chorus never says unbreakable. It says it takes more than it used to, and it's going to take more tomorrow.

He wanted this one to lift. Most of the first five songs are heavy, and he asked for something honest that didn't leave you on the floor. So the arrangement builds the whole way: one guitar and one voice at the start, a crowd of voices by the end.

The line that was already a lyric

The bridge came out of his mouth before anyone wrote it down. People ask him what it was like over there and he still doesn't have the words. He never named the thing he carries. He was saving room for theirs. If you've ever been responsible for other people, you know exactly what that means.

The names

Ask him why they all went over and he'll tell you that's above his pay. Ask him who was in the room and he'll give you every face. Nobody misses how it was. They miss who they were in it, and who they were in it with. Those names went in the wet cement the day he made it home, and they aren't going anywhere.

More stories behind the songs

Every song on this site started as one true thing a veteran handed over. No names go on the songs, ever. If you've got a story, it costs nothing, you approve every word, and everything the music earns goes to veteran suicide prevention.
